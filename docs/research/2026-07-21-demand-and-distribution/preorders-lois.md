# BOTTOM LINE

“Sell it before you build it” is possible, but the popular version is overstated. There is no credible cohort study showing that unknown solo founders with no audience routinely get pre-orders for unbuilt B2B dev tools. The best available data is from established B2B sellers, so treat it as an outbound-effort reality check, not a forecast for you.

The only signals that justify building are:

| Signal | Count as demand? | Count as revenue? |
|---|---:|---:|
| “Sounds useful,” waitlist, intro | No | No |
| Non-binding LOI | Weakly | No |
| Design-partner access/weekly time | Somewhat | No |
| Signed paid pilot / paid discovery | Yes | Yes, when paid |
| Deposit or pre-order with real terms | Yes | Cash received; revenue recognition depends on delivery |
| Signed production contract / PO with payment obligation | Strong | Contracted revenue, not cash |

A useful founder rule—not a published industry cutoff—is: do not build the full product after one enthusiastic prospect. Build the smallest delivery path after either (a) one buyer pays, or (b) three unrelated buyers commit to the same priced, tightly defined pilot. Three is evidence of repeatability; one is evidence that this particular buyer may be unusually motivated.

# What the published numbers actually say

## Outbound is a much harsher funnel than founder anecdotes imply

Gong’s analysis of 85 million cold emails found median sellers booked meetings from just **0.3% of emails**—about **3 meetings per 1,000 emails**. The top decile booked 2.3%, but that is the top decile of professional sellers, not a baseline for a new founder. Median reply rate was 2%; top decile 8%. [Gong’s 85M-email report](https://www.gong.io/files/gong-guide-how-to-master-cold-email-get-the-data-backed-guide-based-on-85-million-emails.pdf)

That has an immediate implication: a hand-picked list of 100 accounts is not a meaningful “market test” if it produces zero meetings. At a 0.3% booked-meeting rate, 100 sends have an expected 0.3 meetings and roughly a **74% chance of zero**. Even 300 sends have about a 41% chance of zero. A thousand well-targeted sends with zero meetings is much more concerning—but still diagnoses the combination of list, message, channel, and offer, not necessarily the product idea.

Cold calling is similarly brutal at the median. Gong/30MPC’s 300M-call analysis found average reps connected on 5.4% of dials, then converted 4.6% of conversations to meetings: approximately **2 meetings from 800 dials/month**. Top-quartile reps reached 18 meetings from the same volume. [Gong’s analysis](https://www.gong.io/blog/the-hidden-power-of-cold-calling-insights-from-300m-calls)

Use these as instrumentation targets:

- 1,000 targeted email sends: median external benchmark ≈ 3 meetings.
- 800 calls: median external benchmark ≈ 2 meetings.
- Zero meetings after 100–300 touches: **not decisive**.
- Zero meetings after 1,000 carefully targeted touches, multiple message angles, and direct follow-up: treat the offer/ICP as failed until you can identify a concrete reason it should not apply.

These figures come from sales-tool customers and professional reps, so an unknown founder may do worse on initial trust. On the other hand, a founder with unusually sharp ICP knowledge can do better than generic SDR outbound.

## “How many conversations to one commitment?”

No high-quality published dataset tracks “cold founder conversation → paid pre-product commitment” across B2B startups. The closest defensible numbers are later-funnel, existing-company benchmarks.

The 2024 SaaSiest survey of **143 northern-European B2B SaaS companies** reported an average **28.7% opportunity win rate**—roughly **3–4 qualified opportunities per closed deal**. That is not “3–4 discovery calls per deal”: an opportunity is already qualified and usually has budget, process, fit, and stakeholder engagement. [SaaSiest report](https://saasiest.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/SaaSiest-Benchmark-Report.pdf)

Do not multiply the Gong meeting rate by this win rate and call it a forecast. The datasets use different companies and funnel definitions. The honest model is:

`prospects contacted → replies → meetings → qualified opportunities → paid pilots → production contracts`

You must measure your own stage definitions. For an unbuilt product, the key conversion is not meeting-to-close; it is:

`qualified buyer who heard the price → signed commercial action`

A practical threshold:

- Less than 20 genuinely qualified conversations: insufficient evidence either way.
- 20 qualified conversations, zero buyer willing to take a priced next step—paid discovery, pilot, deposit, procurement review, or signed conditional order: do not build the full product. Change ICP, problem, or offer.
- Three priced commitments from unaffiliated buyers: enough signal to build the narrow delivery path, not enough to claim product-market fit.

Those thresholds are operating rules, not validated industry benchmarks.

# Deal sizes and sales-cycle reality

There is no reliable public benchmark for “pre-revenue developer-tool paid-pilot price.” Claims such as “charge $10k for every pilot” are usually sales advice, not data.

Useful adjacent benchmarks:

- SaaSiest’s 143-company survey reported average sales cycles of **2.4 months for sub-€5k ACV**, **4.5 months for €5k–€10k**, **5.6 months for €10k–€25k**, and **6.8 months for €50k–€100k**. These are mature B2B SaaS deals, not founder pilots. [Report](https://saasiest.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/SaaSiest-Benchmark-Report.pdf)
- SaaS Capital’s 2025 analysis of 1,000+ private B2B SaaS respondents reported median ACV of **$26,265** overall; for companies above $1M ARR, bootstrapped firms had median ACV **$23,391** and equity-backed firms **$35,761**. This is explicitly not a pre-revenue benchmark. [SaaS Capital](https://www.saas-capital.com/blog-posts/what-is-the-average-deal-size-for-private-saas-companies/)
- ChartMogul’s 2,500-company dataset finds B2B companies commonly add sales support around a $100 average sale price and increasingly use sales as purchase complexity rises; it also reports B2B trial conversion around day seven at 2.5%, versus 16% for B2C. [ChartMogul GTM report](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-go-to-market-report/)

For a solo founder, I would test this offer—not present it as an industry norm:

- 30–45-day paid pilot.
- One use case, one team, one measurable before/after metric.
- $2k–$5k fee, fully credited against a $12k–$30k annual contract if they proceed.
- Fixed maximum implementation effort and explicit exclusions.
- A production price and conversion meeting date agreed before pilot start.

The point of a low-five-figure annual target is not that it is “typical”; it is that it can plausibly justify founder-led onboarding while remaining below a large enterprise transformation. If your buyer’s avoided cost is only a few hundred dollars monthly, do not force an enterprise-pilot motion—use self-serve or a smaller paid implementation.

# Playbooks that create actual instrumentation

## 1. Paid pilot: the best pre-build test

Offer an outcome, not access to hypothetical software:

> “We will reduce X in this workflow for Y team during 30 days. You provide Z data/access and one weekly working session. Fee is $3,000, credited against the annual price of $18,000. Success is A, B, and C. At day 30, we decide production against the pre-agreed criteria.”

Required terms:

- Buyer, economic owner, champion, users.
- One bounded workflow and baseline.
- Objective success criteria.
- Data/security constraints.
- Start/end date; weekly checkpoints.
- Pilot fee, payment date, refund/cancellation terms.
- Production price, intended contract shape, and conversion meeting before the pilot starts.

Threshold: **payment plus a named buyer-owned success metric**. A “free pilot” is not a test of willingness to pay. A paid pilot that cannot name the eventual production budget owner is still fragile.

No credible public source supplies a general pilot-to-production conversion rate. Treat any claim like “structured pilots convert at 60–70%” as unverified unless the publisher discloses population, denominator, timeframe, and definition of conversion.

## 2. Paid discovery: use when the product cannot responsibly be promised yet

Sell a 1–2 week technical assessment that produces a concrete artifact: integration map, baseline, prototype output, security assessment, and an implementation plan.

Use this when the buyer has real pain but you cannot honestly commit to a production outcome yet. Price it as work, not as a token. Credit it toward the subsequent pilot only if that is part of the offer.

Threshold: a paid assessment is demand for your expertise and problem understanding; it is not yet proof that the eventual software will sell. Require the assessment to end with an explicit go/no-go production decision at a stated price.

## 3. Pre-order/deposit: strongest when the buyer can buy without enterprise procurement

A pre-order should specify:

- Exact buyer/use case.
- Price and amount paid now.
- Delivery window.
- What is refundable and under what conditions.
- What the buyer receives if the product changes or is not delivered.

Threshold: **money transferred by a real buyer with authority**. A refundable $50 reservation from a friendly founder is weaker than a $2,000 invoice paid from an operating budget. There is no evidence-based universal minimum deposit percentage.

For B2B developer tools, a deposit is often less practical than a paid pilot because buyers need to test security, integration, and reliability before they can justify an annual commitment. Sell the initial delivery, not vaporware access.

## 4. Design partner: useful, but do not call it revenue

A valid design partner gives:

- Named executive sponsor.
- At least one weekly working session.
- Access to representative workflow/data, subject to appropriate agreements.
- Permission to test against a real baseline.
- A written intended production price and buying decision date.

Threshold: if there is no money, no production price, and no buying date, it is user research—not commercial validation. That can still be valuable, but it should not be used to justify revenue projections or months of product work.

## 5. LOI: a negotiating artifact, not a conversion metric

A non-binding LOI commonly records intent while preserving the right not to buy. It can help force specificity—buyer, scope, price, conditions, intended timing—but it is not cash or a forecastable booking.

Legally, enforceability depends on wording and context. Preliminary agreements can be non-binding, partly binding, or unintentionally treated as contracts; explicit separation of binding and non-binding clauses matters. [SSRN legal overview](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5359187), [Practical Law/Hogan Lovells overview](https://www.hoganlovells.com/-/media/project/english-site/our-thinking/publication-embedded-media/pdfs/2025/contract-formation-and-enforcement-in-the-united-states-overview.pdf)

Threshold: count a non-binding LOI as **zero revenue**. Upgrade it by obtaining either a paid SOW, a binding order contingent only on defined acceptance criteria, or a PO/payment commitment. Have local counsel review any document you expect to bind either party.

# A 30-day zero-audience experiment

1. Pick one ICP narrow enough to list 100–300 companies manually: same stack, workflow, trigger, and buyer.
2. Write one expensive failure in their language and one measurable promise. Do not lead with “AI platform” or feature inventory.
3. Contact 1,000 people/accounts in a controlled sequence if the market is large enough; use email plus calls. Measure booked meeting, qualified problem, price discussed, commercial action—not opens.
4. On discovery, ask for the current workaround, cost of failure, decision owner, available budget path, and what would make them unwilling to pay.
5. Present the paid pilot price before writing a custom proposal.
6. Do not build custom product work until payment or a signed, enforceable paid commitment exists.
7. Publish only aggregate funnel numbers after 30 days: touches, meetings, qualified opportunities, price-presented opportunities, paid commitments, cash collected.

The most useful binary test is not “would you use this?” It is: “Will you authorize this narrowly scoped paid intervention now, at this price, with this success test?”

# WHAT I COULD NOT FIND

- A reliable published conversion rate from **B2B startup LOI → paid contract**, especially for pre-product developer/AI tools. I found legal analysis of LOIs, but no credible multi-company revenue-conversion dataset.
- A reliable benchmark for **paid pilot → production conversion** with disclosed sample, definitions, and methodology. Many vendors claim 40–70%+ for structured pilots; most provide no auditable underlying data.
- A reliable benchmark for **pre-revenue B2B developer-tool pilot price**, deposit size, or design-partner compensation. Existing SaaS ACV surveys cover operating companies, not unbuilt products.
- A study isolating founders with **zero audience, zero reputation, and zero network** and measuring their success at selling before building. The outbound datasets are primarily from established sales teams and tool customers, so they likely overstate trust and operational support available to a solo unknown founder.
- A defensible universal answer to “how many conversations per commitment.” Published figures use incompatible stages—dials, replies, meetings, qualified opportunities, proposals, and closed-won—and startups define them inconsistently.
- Evidence that LOIs predict revenue well enough to use in a forecast. The available evidence supports treating them as a way to sharpen a conversation, not as a revenue instrument.
